Clause-by-Clause Findings

§7
Liability Cap — $10,000 Is Dangerously Low
A hard cap of $10,000 on total liability means that even a minor breach—sharing a deck with the wrong person—leaves the injured party with virtually no financial remedy if real damages are larger. For any deal with commercial value above five figures this cap would be inadequate.
Recommended fix: Tie the cap to a multiple of contract value, prior 12-month revenue, or at minimum $500K–$1M. Consider carving out indemnification for third-party IP claims.
High
§7
Consequential Damages Waiver — No Injunctive-Relief Carve-Out
Excluding indirect/consequential damages without carving out injunctive or equitable relief is standard drafting error for NDAs. Trade-secret breaches are inherently consequential (lost deals, competitive harm). Without an express carve-out, the non-breaching party may struggle to obtain a temporary restraining order and would be left with only a $10,000 damages cap—effectively no protection.
Recommended fix: Add: "Notwithstanding the foregoing, nothing herein limits either party's right to seek injunctive or other equitable relief for actual or threatened breach of confidentiality obligations."
High
§1
Definition of Confidential Information — Overly Broad, No Exclusions
"Any non-public business or technical information" is so wide it could capture publicly available information that one party hadn't publicly released yet, or information independently developed by the other party. Standard practice includes exclusions for: (a) information already known; (b) publicly available information; (c) independently developed; (d) received from a third party without restriction.
Recommended fix: Add the four standard exclusions as a separate sub-clause to §1.
Medium
§6
Arbitration Clause — Incomplete
The clause selects binding arbitration in Wilmington, DE under Delaware law but omits: the arbitration rules/administrator (AAA? JAMS? Ad hoc?), the number of arbitrators, language, and confidentiality of the arbitration itself. An incomplete clause can be unenforceable or lead to expensive preliminary litigation about procedure.
Recommended fix: Specify the administering body (e.g., "under AAA Commercial Rules"), number of arbitrators (1 for disputes <$250K), and that the arbitration proceeding is itself confidential.
Medium
§5
Termination — No Return/Destruction of Materials Obligation
The clause allows either party to terminate with 30 days notice and says obligations survive, but there is no obligation to return or certifiably destroy the other party's confidential materials upon termination. Without this, a party can terminate and retain all disclosed information for the remaining 5-year obligation window.
Recommended fix: Add a return-or-destroy clause triggered by either termination or written request, with certification within 10 business days.
Medium
§3
5-Year Survival Period — Standard but Consider Trade Secrets
Five years post-disclosure is a commonly accepted term and reasonable for most business information. However, if either party is disclosing trade secrets (as opposed to ordinary confidential information), the Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) provides protection without a time limit—a fixed 5-year cap could inadvertently waive perpetual protection.
Recommended fix: Add a carve-out: "…provided that obligations with respect to trade secrets shall survive for so long as such information constitutes a trade secret under applicable law."
Low
§2
Standard of Care — Acceptable Dual-Standard Language
"Same degree of care as its own, no less than reasonable care" is the gold-standard formulation. It prevents a party with low internal standards from using those as a defense, while still grounding the obligation in an objective reasonable-care floor. No changes needed.
Low
§4
No-License Clause — Standard; Note the Gap on Compelled Disclosure
The no-IP-license clause is correct boilerplate. However, the agreement has no clause addressing what happens when a party receives a subpoena or regulatory demand to disclose. Without a compelled-disclosure protocol (notify the other party promptly, cooperate to seek a protective order), the receiving party has no contractual hook to resist and the disclosing party gets no advance warning.
Recommended fix: Add a compelled-disclosure provision requiring prompt written notice to the other party and cooperation in seeking a protective order.

Strengths & Weaknesses at a Glance

What the Agreement Gets Right

  • Mutual obligations — neither party holds a one-sided position
  • Dual standard of care (own + reasonable care floor)
  • Clear no-license provision protecting IP ownership
  • Written amendment requirement — prevents oral modification
  • Entire-agreement clause reduces extrinsic evidence risk
  • Specific forum (Wilmington DE) reduces venue disputes

What Needs Attention Before Signing

  • $10,000 liability cap far too low for most commercial deals
  • No injunctive-relief carve-out undermines enforceability
  • Definition lacks standard four exclusions
  • Incomplete arbitration clause — no rules or administrator named
  • No return/destroy obligation on termination
  • No compelled-disclosure protocol
  • 5-year term may inadvertently cap trade-secret protection

Prioritized Recommendations

  1. Add injunctive-relief carve-out to §7 — without this, the damages waiver + cap combination could leave a breached party with no practical remedy. Highest priority.
  2. Raise or restructure the liability cap — $10,000 is rarely commercially appropriate. Negotiate a realistic cap (e.g., greater of $500K or 12-month contract value).
  3. Add standard CI exclusions to §1 — public knowledge, prior knowledge, independent development, and third-party disclosure without restriction.
  4. Specify arbitration administrator and rules in §6 — name AAA or JAMS, set arbitrator count, and confirm the proceeding is confidential.
  5. Add return/destroy provision — trigger on termination or written demand; require certification within 10 business days.
  6. Add compelled-disclosure protocol — prompt notice + cooperation on protective orders.
  7. Carve out trade secrets from the 5-year survival cap — protect perpetual trade-secret status under DTSA.
